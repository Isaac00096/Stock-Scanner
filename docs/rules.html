Trading Rules

Based on H.M. Gartley, Profits in the Stock Market (1935), mainly the summary chapter, adapted to a small CFD account. ← dashboard · setup results
Gartley's first rule: prove it on paper before using real money.
He treats paper trading as a required step and expects learning to take many months of spare-time practice, roughly a year and a half in his view. Our minimum: paper trade the dashboard signals on a demo account and log every trade in the journal until you have 30+ trades whose results match the validation page.

Before any trade

  1. Pick one timeframe and stick to it. Gartley's advice for someone with another job: trade the intermediate trend (moves lasting weeks to months), not short swings. Chasing small moves while busy elsewhere is, in his words, fatal to the capital account. That's why the dashboard uses daily charts and overnight limit orders, not the opening rush.
  2. Check the trend first. Major trend: is the stock above its 200-day average? Intermediate: above its 25-day average (3-day lead)? Only then look for a buying spot.
  3. Trade the leaders. Buy stocks doing better than the market (the "market leaders only" setups), in groups that are leading. Avoid one-off popular stocks.
  4. Avoid the "dogs" and the sleepers. Skip erratic stocks under $10 (the scanner already does) and slow-moving defensive stocks. Moderately priced, actively traded stocks of sound companies are best.
  5. Only take trades worth the risk. A 1-to-1 trade (risk R10 to make R10) is gambling to Gartley. His guide: don't risk 10% unless about 30% looks possible, roughly 3 to 1. Check the stop and target on the dashboard before placing an order.
  6. Only act on a tested setup. He warns against trusting the newest tool you've learned before testing it. Use only setups marked validated.

Managing money

  1. Never trade with more than half of your total savings.
  2. Don't borrow (no margin). Gartley avoided margin except in rare high-conviction moments. Trade Nation's 20:1 leverage is margin, so size each position as if there were no leverage: the position's full value should not be more than your account.
  3. Spread the risk. Even a small account should hold 3–5 positions, not one. 5–10 is ideal.
  4. Cut losses while they're small. Letting losses grow is the mark of an amateur. Always set the stop. Never hold a losing trade hoping to "get out even", and never take a long shot to win back a loss.
  5. Let winners run, but bank part of them. Once a trade is up about 20%, lock in half to two-thirds of the profit (sell part, or move the stop up).
  6. Keep a reserve. Put aside a fifth to a third of every good profit, and don't dip into it after losing streaks.

Realistic expectations

Yearly goal18–24% a year on the money traded, without borrowing. More in good years, less in lean ones. On R100, that's about R20 a year: this account is for learning, not income.
Win rateBeing right 60% of the time was his goal, not 80–100%. Profits come from small losses and bigger wins.
TradesFor an intermediate-trend trader: a handful of well-chosen positions, not daily activity.

Discipline

  1. Every trade is an act of reason. A hasty trade is usually an emotional one.
  2. Keep your own counsel. Learn facts from anyone, but don't take tips or give them.
  3. Be patient. There will always be another opportunity; don't dwell on missed ones.
  4. Expect to be wrong often. Don't let a day or two of bad action shake a well-reasoned position, but respect the stop.

Gartley's methods in this tool

222 buying spot (Ch. VIII): he claimed it works about 8 times in 10 — tested as two setups (limit order and "on the turn").
Breakout above a previous top (Ch. VIII): he found about 7 in 10 led to a worthwhile move — tested with a volume condition.
25-day moving average with a 3-day lead (Ch. XI): his best result in tests on 1919–1933 data — tested on all stocks, and on market leaders only.
Better-than-market stocks (Ch. XVII): used as a filter on the "market leaders only" setups.

His win rates came from 1930s market averages. The validation page shows how each method holds up on the last 10 years of NYSE stocks, including the recent 3 years it was never tuned on.
